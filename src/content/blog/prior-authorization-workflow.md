Prior authorization — sometimes called pre-certification or pre-approval — is the requirement that a health plan approve certain services before they are provided. For practices, it is one of the most time-consuming administrative tasks, and a missing authorization is one of the hardest denials to overturn.

A reliable workflow turns authorizations from a source of lost revenue and delayed care into a predictable, trackable process.

## Which services usually need authorization?

Requirements vary by payer and plan, but authorization is commonly required for:

- Advanced imaging such as MRI, CT, PET, and nuclear cardiology
- Elective surgeries and many outpatient procedures
- Specialty and injectable drugs
- Durable medical equipment
- Physical, occupational, and speech therapy beyond a set number of visits
- Behavioral health services, sleep studies, and genetic testing
- Inpatient admissions and post-acute care

Many plans also delegate authorization for certain services to benefit managers, such as radiology or pharmacy benefit managers, which have their own portals and requirements.

## Step 1: Identify requirements at scheduling

The earlier you know an authorization is needed, the less likely the service is to be delayed or denied. Build checks into scheduling:

- Maintain a list of high-volume services that commonly require authorization by payer
- Check payer portals or tools for the specific CPT and diagnosis codes
- Flag appointments that need authorization so they are not confirmed until approval is received

## Step 2: Gather complete clinical documentation

Most authorization delays happen because the request is incomplete. Before submitting, collect:

- Patient demographics and insurance details
- CPT/HCPCS codes, units, and the expected date of service
- ICD-10 diagnosis codes that support medical necessity
- Relevant clinical notes, previous treatments tried, test results, and imaging
- The ordering and rendering provider and the site of service

Review the payer’s medical policy for the service. If it requires, for example, six weeks of conservative therapy before an MRI, the request should show that history clearly.

## Step 3: Submit and track

Submit through the payer’s portal, an electronic prior authorization tool, or fax, depending on the payer. Then track every request in a single log or work queue with:

- Submission date and method
- Reference or case number
- Current status and next follow-up date
- Approved codes, units, date range, and site of service

Follow up on pending requests before they hold up the schedule.

## Step 4: Match the service to the approval

An authorization is only valid for what was approved. Before the service:

- Confirm the CPT codes and number of units match the approval
- Check that the date of service falls within the approved date range
- Verify that the location and rendering provider match

If the planned service changes — for example, a surgeon adds a procedure during surgery — contact the payer promptly. Many payers allow updates within a short window after the service.

## Step 5: Handle denials and peer-to-peer reviews

If an authorization request is denied:

1. Read the denial reason carefully — is it missing information or a true medical necessity decision?
2. Submit additional documentation if information was missing
3. Request a **peer-to-peer review**, where the treating physician discusses the case with the payer’s medical reviewer
4. File a formal appeal within the deadline if the denial stands

## The CMS prior authorization rule

The CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule (CMS-0057-F) applies to Medicare Advantage organizations, Medicaid and CHIP programs, and qualified health plans on the federal exchanges. Beginning in **2026**, these payers generally must:

- Make decisions on **expedited** requests within **72 hours** and **standard** requests within **7 calendar days**
- Provide a **specific reason** when they deny a request
- Publicly report prior authorization metrics

Additional requirements for electronic prior authorization interfaces (APIs) are scheduled to take effect in 2027. These rules should make the process faster and more transparent, but they do not remove the need for complete, well-documented requests. Commercial plans not covered by the rule may follow different timelines, and some states have their own prior authorization laws.

## Measuring your authorization process

Track these indicators monthly:

- Number of authorization requests and approval rate
- Average turnaround time by payer
- Denials for missing or invalid authorization (CARC CO-197 or CO-15)
- Services rescheduled because of authorization delays
