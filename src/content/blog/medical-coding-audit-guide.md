A medical coding audit compares what was billed with what was documented in the medical record. It answers a simple but important question: **are we coding accurately?**

Accurate coding protects revenue in both directions. Undercoding leaves money uncollected for work that was done. Overcoding, or billing without supporting documentation, creates compliance risk and the possibility of repayments. A regular audit program catches both.

## Why practices audit

- **Find lost revenue** from undercoded visits, missed procedures, and missing modifiers
- **Reduce compliance risk** before a payer or government audit finds the problem
- **Improve documentation** through targeted provider education
- **Reduce denials** caused by coding errors
- **Evaluate new providers, coders, or services**
- **Meet compliance program expectations**, since regular auditing and monitoring is one of the core elements of an effective compliance program described by the HHS Office of Inspector General

## Types of coding audits

### Prospective (pre-bill) audits

Claims are reviewed **before** submission, so errors are corrected before the payer sees them. This is useful for new providers, new services, or areas with known problems. It slows submission slightly but prevents incorrect claims.

### Retrospective (post-payment) audits

Claims that have already been paid are reviewed. This shows historical patterns and is the most common type of routine audit. If overpayments are found, they must be handled appropriately — Medicare, for example, requires identified overpayments to be reported and returned within set timeframes.

### Focused audits

These target a specific area, such as E/M levels, [modifier 25](/resources/modifier-25-explained) use, a high-denial procedure, or a particular payer.

## How an audit works

1. **Define the scope.** Which providers, services, payers, and dates of service?
2. **Select a sample.** A common starting point is 10–20 encounters per provider, chosen randomly or targeted at high-risk areas.
3. **Review documentation.** Auditors compare the medical record with the codes billed: E/M level, procedure codes, diagnosis codes, modifiers, and units.
4. **Score accuracy.** Each encounter is rated as correct, undercoded, or overcoded, with the dollar impact.
5. **Report findings.** A clear report summarizes accuracy rates, trends, financial impact, and specific examples.
6. **Educate.** Providers and coders receive feedback with practical recommendations.
7. **Follow up.** A later review confirms whether accuracy has improved.

## What auditors look for

- **E/M levels:** Whether medical decision-making or time supports the level billed. See [CPT code 99214 explained](/resources/cpt-99214-explained).
- **Diagnosis coding:** Specificity, correct sequencing, and support for medical necessity
- **Procedure coding:** Correct codes, units, and bundling
- **Modifiers:** Whether modifiers such as 25, 59, and the X modifiers are supported
- **Documentation requirements:** Signatures, dates, orders, and time statements
- **Templates and cloned notes:** Whether notes reflect the specific visit
- **Missed charges:** Services documented but not billed

## Interpreting results

Many organizations target **95% coding accuracy** or higher. Lower accuracy does not necessarily mean wrongdoing — it often reflects documentation habits, template design, or misunderstanding of guidelines. The key is to understand the pattern:

- **Consistent undercoding** usually means providers are not getting credit for their work, often because documentation does not capture their decision-making
- **Consistent overcoding** requires prompt education and may require review of past claims
- **Random errors** often point to workflow or system issues

## Making audits part of your routine

A sustainable audit program might include:

- An **annual** baseline audit of every provider
- **Quarterly** focused reviews of high-risk areas
- **Prospective review** for new providers during their first months
- **Targeted audits** whenever denial trends or payer requests point to a problem

Good documentation supports good coding. Practices whose providers are overwhelmed by charting sometimes pair audits with [medical scribing](/services/medical-scribing) support, so notes are complete and coding-ready.
