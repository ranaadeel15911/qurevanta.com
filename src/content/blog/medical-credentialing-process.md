Before a health plan will pay a provider’s claims, the provider must be **credentialed** — verified as qualified — and **enrolled** with that plan. For new providers, new practices, and practices adding staff, credentialing is often the single biggest cause of delayed revenue.

## Credentialing versus enrollment versus contracting

These terms are often used interchangeably, but they describe different steps:

- **Credentialing** is the verification of a provider’s qualifications: education, training, licensure, board certification, work history, malpractice history, and sanctions.
- **Enrollment** is registering the provider with a payer so claims can be submitted and paid.
- **Contracting** establishes the agreement and reimbursement rates between the practice and a commercial payer.

All three usually need to be complete before a provider is considered in-network and can be paid at contracted rates.

## Step 1: Gather documents

Start with a complete credentialing file for each provider. Typical items include:

- Individual NPI (and the group NPI)
- State medical or professional license(s)
- DEA registration and any state controlled substance registration
- Board certification
- Education and training certificates (medical school, residency, fellowship)
- Current CV with month and year for all work history, with explanations for any gaps
- Malpractice insurance certificate and claims history
- Hospital privileges, if applicable
- Government-issued ID and Social Security number
- Practice information: tax ID, W-9, addresses, and bank details for EFT

Keeping these documents organized — with expiration dates tracked — saves weeks later.

## Step 2: Complete the CAQH ProView profile

Most commercial payers use **CAQH ProView**, a free online database where providers enter credentialing information once and authorize payers to access it. To be effective, the profile must be:

- Complete, with all required documents uploaded
- **Attested** — providers must re-attest regularly (currently every 120 days) or payers may not be able to access it
- Authorized for each payer you are applying to

An expired attestation is one of the most common and easily avoided causes of delay.

## Step 3: Enroll with Medicare and Medicaid

Government programs have their own enrollment systems. Medicare enrollment is completed through **PECOS** or CMS-855 forms and processed by your regional Medicare Administrative Contractor. Medicaid enrollment is managed by each state, and many states also require separate credentialing with Medicaid managed care plans. For a detailed example, read [how long Medicare credentialing takes in Florida](/resources/medicare-credentialing-florida).

## Step 4: Apply to commercial payers

Decide which commercial and Medicare Advantage plans to join based on your patient population and market. For each payer:

1. Confirm the panel is open in your specialty and area
2. Submit the application, usually referencing the CAQH profile
3. Follow up regularly — applications can sit without action for weeks
4. Review and sign the contract and fee schedule

Before signing, review reimbursement rates carefully. See our guide on [preparing for payer contract negotiations](/resources/payer-contract-negotiation).

## Step 5: Load and test

Once approved, note each payer’s effective date and provider ID, update your practice management system, and complete [ERA and EDI enrollment](/services/era-edi-setup) so claims and payments flow electronically.

## How long does credentialing take?

Timelines vary widely:

- **Medicare:** commonly 30–90 days once a complete application is submitted
- **Medicaid:** varies by state, often 30–120 days plus managed care credentialing
- **Commercial plans:** commonly 60–180 days for credentialing and contracting

**Start at least 90 to 120 days before a provider’s start date** whenever possible.

## Common causes of delay

- Incomplete applications or missing signatures
- Unexplained gaps in work history
- Expired documents or CAQH attestation
- Information that does not match across NPPES, licenses, and applications
- Slow responses to payer requests
- Not following up on submitted applications

## Re-credentialing and maintenance

Credentialing is not a one-time event. Commercial payers typically re-credential providers every **three years**, and Medicare requires periodic revalidation. Licenses, DEA registrations, board certifications, and malpractice policies expire on their own schedules. A credentialing calendar with automatic reminders prevents lapses that can interrupt payment.
