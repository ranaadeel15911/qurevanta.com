Every denied claim costs money twice: once in delayed or lost payment, and again in the staff time it takes to rework it. Industry surveys regularly report that a meaningful share of claims are denied on first submission, and that a large portion of those denials were preventable.

The good news is that most denials fall into a small number of categories. If you understand the ten below — and the claim adjustment reason codes (CARCs) that usually accompany them — you can prevent most of them before they happen.

## 1. Patient not eligible on the date of service

**Typical codes:** CO-27 (expenses incurred after coverage terminated), CO-26 (expenses incurred before coverage), CO-31 (patient cannot be identified as our insured)

Coverage changes more often than most patients realize — new jobs, Medicaid redeterminations, and annual plan changes all play a part. **Prevention:** verify eligibility before every visit, not just the first one. See our guide to [insurance eligibility verification](/resources/insurance-eligibility-verification).

## 2. Missing or incorrect information

**Typical code:** CO-16 (claim lacks information or has submission/billing errors), usually with a remark code identifying the missing item

Wrong subscriber IDs, misspelled names, missing referring provider NPIs, and invalid dates are simple mistakes that stop payment. **Prevention:** registration checklists, front-desk scanning of insurance cards, and claim scrubbing before submission.

## 3. No prior authorization

**Typical code:** CO-197 (precertification/authorization/notification absent), CO-15 (authorization number missing or invalid)

Imaging, procedures, specialty drugs, and some therapies require approval in advance. Denials for missing authorization are among the hardest to overturn. **Prevention:** a reliable [prior authorization workflow](/resources/prior-authorization-workflow) that checks requirements at scheduling.

## 4. Duplicate claim

**Typical code:** CO-18 (exact duplicate claim/service)

Duplicates often happen when staff resubmit a claim that has not been paid instead of checking its status or submitting a properly coded corrected claim. **Prevention:** check claim status before resubmitting, and use the correct claim frequency code for corrections.

## 5. Timely filing limit exceeded

**Typical code:** CO-29 (the time limit for filing has expired)

Every payer sets a deadline for submitting claims. Once it has passed, the claim is usually unrecoverable unless you can prove timely submission. **Prevention:** submit claims within days of service and monitor rejected claims that never reached the payer. Read more about [timely filing limits](/resources/timely-filing-limits).

## 6. Service not medically necessary

**Typical code:** CO-50 (non-covered services because this is not deemed a medical necessity by the payer)

The diagnosis on the claim does not support the service under the payer’s coverage policy. **Prevention:** check national and local coverage determinations and commercial medical policies for high-volume services, and link each procedure to the diagnosis that supports it.

## 7. Diagnosis inconsistent with the procedure

**Typical code:** CO-11 (the diagnosis is inconsistent with the procedure)

Related to medical necessity, this denial means the diagnosis code does not match the type of service billed — sometimes simply because the wrong diagnosis pointer was used. **Prevention:** coding review and claim scrubbing rules that check diagnosis–procedure combinations.

## 8. Bundled or included service

**Typical code:** CO-97 (benefit included in the payment for another service)

The service is considered part of another procedure. Sometimes this is correct; sometimes a modifier was missing. Our detailed guide to [denial code CO-97](/resources/denial-code-co-97) explains how to resolve it.

## 9. Modifier missing or invalid

**Typical code:** CO-4 (the procedure code is inconsistent with the modifier used, or a required modifier is missing)

Laterality modifiers (RT/LT), professional and technical component modifiers (26/TC), telehealth modifiers, and [modifier 25](/resources/modifier-25-explained) are frequent sources of errors. **Prevention:** payer-specific scrubber rules and provider documentation training.

## 10. Coordination of benefits

**Typical code:** CO-22 (this care may be covered by another payer per coordination of benefits)

When a patient has more than one insurance plan, the payer may deny until the primary plan has processed the claim or the patient updates their coordination-of-benefits information. **Prevention:** ask about other coverage at every registration and confirm the order of benefits during eligibility checks.

## A note on CO-45

Many practices see CO-45 (charge exceeds fee schedule/maximum allowable) on nearly every claim. It is not a denial — it is the contractual write-off between your charge and the allowed amount. However, it is worth monitoring: if the allowed amount is lower than your contract says it should be, you have an underpayment to pursue.

## Building a denial prevention program

1. **Measure your denial rate** by payer, reason, provider, and location. A useful target is to keep initial denials in the low single digits as a percentage of claims.
2. **Group denials by root cause** — front desk, authorization, coding, billing, or payer error.
3. **Fix the upstream process**, not just the claim. If eligibility denials are rising, the solution is at check-in, not in the billing office.
4. **Work denials quickly.** Appeal windows are limited, and success rates fall as claims age.
5. **Review trends monthly** and share them with the people who can change them.
