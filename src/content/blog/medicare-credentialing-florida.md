For a new physician, a new practice, or a provider joining a Florida group, Medicare enrollment is often the first major step before any claims can be paid. The most common question we hear is simple: **how long will it take?**

The honest answer is that a complete, accurate Medicare application in Florida commonly takes **about 30 to 90 days** to process once it reaches the Medicare Administrative Contractor — but the full journey, from preparation to being able to bill, often takes **two to four months**. Errors, missing documents, or development requests can stretch that further.

This guide explains each step and where the time goes.

## Who processes Medicare enrollment in Florida?

Medicare enrollment applications for Florida physicians and other Part B suppliers are processed by **First Coast Service Options**, the Medicare Administrative Contractor (MAC) for Jurisdiction N, which covers Florida, Puerto Rico, and the U.S. Virgin Islands. Applications are submitted through CMS’s online enrollment system, **PECOS** (Provider Enrollment, Chain, and Ownership System), or on paper CMS-855 forms. PECOS is generally faster and easier to track.

## Step-by-step timeline

### Step 1: National Provider Identifier (NPI) — usually days

Every provider needs an individual (Type 1) NPI, and a group or organization needs its own organizational (Type 2) NPI. NPIs are issued through NPPES and are often granted within a few days when applied for online.

### Step 2: Florida license and supporting documents — varies

Medicare requires an active, unrestricted Florida license. If the provider is new to the state, licensing through the Florida Board of Medicine (or the relevant board) can be the longest part of the entire process. You will also need documents such as board certification, DEA registration where applicable, the practice location details, and bank information for electronic funds transfer.

### Step 3: Prepare and submit the CMS-855 applications — 1 to 2 weeks to prepare

Depending on your situation, you may need several applications:

- **CMS-855I** for an individual physician or non-physician practitioner
- **CMS-855B** for a clinic, group practice, or certain other suppliers
- **Reassignment of benefits** (submitted through PECOS or on the CMS-855R) so an individual can bill through a group
- **CMS-588** for electronic funds transfer

Each application requires a signature from the provider and, for groups, an authorized or delegated official. Most delays start here — missing signatures, mismatched addresses, or incomplete ownership information.

### Step 4: MAC processing — commonly 30 to 90 days

Once submitted, First Coast reviews the application, verifies licensure and other information, and may send a **development request** asking for corrections or additional documents. Responding quickly is critical: if a development request is not answered within the deadline (typically 30 days), the application may be rejected and you will have to start again.

Some provider types, including newly enrolling DMEPOS suppliers and certain higher-risk categories, require site visits, which add time.

### Step 5: Approval letter and PTAN — then system setup

When approved, you receive an approval letter with a **Provider Transaction Access Number (PTAN)** and an effective date. Before billing, you also need to:

- Enroll in **electronic data interchange (EDI)** with First Coast to submit claims electronically
- Set up **electronic remittance advice (ERA)** so payments post automatically
- Configure the provider in your practice management system

## Understanding the effective date

The effective date determines which services you can bill. For physicians and non-physician practitioners, Medicare generally sets the effective date as the **later of** the date the application was filed (which the contractor later approves) or the date you started furnishing services at the new location. Medicare also permits limited **retrospective billing** — generally up to 30 days before the effective date in qualifying circumstances (longer in certain emergencies).

The practical lesson: **submit your Medicare application as early as possible** — ideally before the provider’s start date. Waiting until the provider is already seeing patients can mean services that can never be billed.

## What about Florida Medicaid and commercial plans?

Medicare is only one piece of the puzzle. Most Florida practices also need to enroll with:

- **Florida Medicaid**, administered by the Agency for Health Care Administration (AHCA). Most Florida Medicaid recipients receive care through **Statewide Medicaid Managed Care (SMMC)** plans, so providers typically enroll with Florida Medicaid and then credential with each managed care plan separately.
- **Medicare Advantage plans**, which have their own credentialing processes separate from traditional Medicare.
- **Commercial insurers**, which commonly take **60 to 180 days** to credential and contract, and often rely on the provider’s **CAQH ProView** profile.

Because these run in parallel, a credentialing calendar that tracks each payer’s status is essential. Read our broader guide to [the medical credentialing process](/resources/medical-credentialing-process).

## The most common causes of delay

1. **Incomplete applications** — missing practice locations, ownership details, or signatures
2. **Mismatched information** — a name, address, or NPI that does not match NPPES, the license, or the IRS records
3. **Slow responses to development requests**
4. **Wrong application type** — for example, enrolling an individual without the reassignment to the group
5. **CAQH profiles that are not attested** or have expired documents (for commercial plans)

## Keeping enrollment active

Approval is not the end. Medicare requires most providers to **revalidate** their enrollment periodically (generally every five years, more often for some supplier types), and you must report changes such as new practice locations, ownership changes, or changes in bank information within the required timeframes. Missing a revalidation deadline can result in deactivation and a gap in billing privileges.
