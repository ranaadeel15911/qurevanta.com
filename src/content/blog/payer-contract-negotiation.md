Your payer contracts determine how much you are paid for nearly every service you provide. Yet many practices sign their initial contracts and never revisit them, even as costs rise year after year. Contracts that are never renegotiated quietly lose value.

Negotiating with large insurers can feel intimidating, especially for smaller practices. But payers do renegotiate, particularly when a practice comes prepared with data and a clear case.

## Step 1: Gather your contracts

Collect every active payer contract, including amendments and fee schedules. For each, note:

- Effective date, term, and renewal date
- Notice period required to renegotiate or terminate
- How rates are set — a fixed fee schedule, a percentage of Medicare, or the payer’s proprietary schedule
- Whether rates escalate automatically
- Key terms such as timely filing limits, appeal deadlines, and payment timeframes

Many contracts renew automatically. Missing the notice window can lock you into current rates for another year.

## Step 2: Analyze your reimbursement data

Identify where your revenue actually comes from:

1. **Rank payers** by total payments and patient volume
2. **Rank CPT codes** by total revenue — often 20–30 codes account for most of a practice’s income
3. **Compare allowed amounts** for those codes across payers
4. **Express rates as a percentage of Medicare** for easy comparison

This analysis usually reveals that one or two payers pay significantly less than others for the same services. Those are your negotiation priorities. Our [fee schedule analysis](/services/fee-schedule-analysis) service performs this review.

## Step 3: Benchmark against the market

Understand what similar practices in your area are likely paid. Useful reference points include:

- The **Medicare Physician Fee Schedule** for your locality
- Publicly available **price transparency data**, which health plans are required to publish
- Industry surveys and specialty society resources

If a payer is reimbursing you well below comparable practices, that is a strong starting point for negotiation.

## Step 4: Build your value case

Payers want networks that are adequate, high quality, and cost-effective. Show why your practice matters:

- **Access:** Number of providers, locations, extended hours, and new patient availability
- **Network need:** Whether you serve an area or specialty with limited in-network options
- **Quality:** Quality scores, patient satisfaction, and outcome data
- **Cost-effectiveness:** Lower-cost sites of service, reduced hospital admissions, or efficient care patterns
- **Specialized services** that other practices in the network do not offer

Present this in a short, professional proposal.

## Step 5: Make a specific request

Be clear about what you want. Rather than asking for "better rates," propose:

- A specific percentage increase, or rates at a stated percentage of Medicare
- Targeted increases for your highest-volume codes
- Annual escalators tied to an index
- Carve-outs for high-cost supplies or drugs

Submit your request in writing to the provider relations or contracting contact, and keep a record of all communications.

## Step 6: Look beyond price

Rates are not the only terms that affect revenue. Review:

- **Timely filing and appeal deadlines** — longer is better
- **Prompt payment terms** and interest on late payments
- **Prior authorization requirements**
- **Medical record requests and audit rights**
- **Recoupment terms** — how far back and with what notice
- **Termination clauses** and notice periods
- **Fee schedule change provisions** — whether the payer can change rates unilaterally

## Step 7: Be patient and persistent

Negotiations can take months. Payers may decline initial requests or offer smaller increases. Follow up regularly, be prepared to provide additional data, and decide in advance what you will do if the payer will not move. In some cases, practices choose to leave a network — a decision that requires careful analysis of patient impact and revenue.

## After the contract is signed

Load the new fee schedule into your practice management system and **monitor payments** to make sure the payer actually pays the agreed rates.
