You cannot improve what you do not measure. A handful of well-chosen key performance indicators (KPIs) can tell you whether your revenue cycle is healthy, where money is being lost, and whether changes are working.

Below are eight KPIs every practice should track, how to calculate them, and the benchmarks commonly used in the industry. Benchmarks vary by specialty, payer mix, and practice size, so the most important comparison is your own trend over time.

## 1. Days in accounts receivable (A/R)

**What it measures:** How long, on average, it takes to collect payment for services.

**Formula:** Total A/R ÷ average daily gross charges (total gross charges for the last 90 days ÷ 90)

**Common benchmark:** Under **40 days** is often cited as good; under 30 days is excellent for many specialties.

Rising days in A/R is usually the first sign of trouble — slow submission, unworked denials, or payer delays.

## 2. Percentage of A/R over 90 days

**What it measures:** How much of your A/R is old and at risk of becoming uncollectible.

**Formula:** A/R older than 90 days ÷ total A/R × 100

**Common benchmark:** Below **15–20%** for insurance A/R.

Old A/R loses value quickly. Claims approaching appeal and filing deadlines need priority attention. Read about [A/R follow-up best practices](/resources/ar-follow-up-best-practices).

## 3. Net collection rate

**What it measures:** The percentage of the money you were contractually entitled to that you actually collected.

**Formula:** Payments ÷ (charges − contractual adjustments) × 100, over the same period

**Common benchmark:** **95% or higher.**

This is arguably the single most important billing KPI. A low net collection rate means money that was owed was never collected — due to denials, write-offs, timely filing, or uncollected patient balances.

## 4. Gross collection rate

**What it measures:** Payments as a percentage of total charges.

**Formula:** Payments ÷ total charges × 100

This number depends mostly on how your fee schedule compares with payer allowables, so it is less useful for judging billing performance. It can, however, help when reviewing your [fee schedule](/services/fee-schedule-analysis).

## 5. Denial rate

**What it measures:** The share of claims denied by payers on first submission.

**Formula:** Claims denied ÷ claims submitted × 100 (also tracked by dollar value)

**Common benchmark:** Under **5–10%**, with many well-run practices aiming for the low single digits.

Always break denials down by reason, payer, and provider. The total rate tells you there is a problem; the breakdown tells you where. See [the most common denial reasons](/resources/common-claim-denial-reasons).

## 6. Clean claim rate

**What it measures:** The percentage of claims accepted and paid without rework on the first submission.

**Formula:** Claims accepted on first submission ÷ total claims × 100

**Common benchmark:** **95% or higher.** Learn more in our guide to [improving your clean claim rate](/resources/clean-claim-rate).

## 7. First-pass resolution rate

**What it measures:** The share of claims that are fully resolved — paid or correctly adjusted — after the first submission.

**Formula:** Claims resolved on first pass ÷ total claims × 100

**Common benchmark:** **90% or higher.**

This KPI reflects the combined quality of registration, coding, and claim submission.

## 8. Charge lag

**What it measures:** The time between the date of service and when the charge is entered or the claim is submitted.

**Formula:** Average days from date of service to claim submission

**Common benchmark:** Ideally **within a few days**.

Charge lag delays every other metric. Long lags are often caused by unsigned notes, coding backlogs, or missing charge information.

## Bonus: patient collection rate and cost to collect

As high-deductible plans grow, **patient collection rate** (patient payments ÷ patient responsibility) has become just as important as insurance performance. **Cost to collect** (total billing costs ÷ total collections) helps you compare in-house and outsourced models — see our [cost comparison](/resources/in-house-vs-outsourced-medical-billing-cost).

## Turning KPIs into action

1. **Choose a consistent reporting period** — usually monthly, with a rolling three-month view.
2. **Review trends**, not single months. One unusual month is noise; three in a row is a signal.
3. **Assign ownership.** Each KPI should have a person responsible for understanding and improving it.
4. **Drill down.** Look at KPIs by payer, provider, location, and service line.
5. **Act on what you find**, and measure whether it worked.
