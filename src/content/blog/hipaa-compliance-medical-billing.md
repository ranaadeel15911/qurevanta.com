Medical billing involves protected health information (PHI) at every step: patient demographics, insurance details, diagnoses, and procedures. Whether billing is done in-house or by an outside company, the **Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)** sets the rules for how that information must be protected.

This article summarizes the essentials for billing operations. It is general information, not legal advice.

## Who HIPAA applies to

- **Covered entities:** Health care providers that conduct standard electronic transactions (such as electronic claims), health plans, and health care clearinghouses.
- **Business associates:** Organizations that create, receive, maintain, or transmit PHI on behalf of a covered entity — including billing companies, coding services, IT vendors, and cloud software providers.

Business associates are directly liable for complying with the HIPAA Security Rule and certain provisions of the Privacy Rule.

## Business associate agreements

Before a practice shares PHI with a billing company or other vendor, the two parties must sign a **business associate agreement (BAA)**. The BAA describes how the vendor may use and disclose PHI, requires appropriate safeguards, and requires the vendor to report breaches. Business associates must also have BAAs with their own subcontractors that handle PHI.

Practices should keep an inventory of all vendors with access to PHI and confirm that each has a current BAA.

## The Privacy Rule and billing

The Privacy Rule allows PHI to be used and disclosed for **payment** activities — such as submitting claims, verifying eligibility, and collecting balances — without patient authorization. However, the **minimum necessary standard** applies: staff should access and share only the information needed for the task.

Practical examples:

- Billing staff need access to diagnoses and procedures, but usually not full clinical notes, except when supporting an appeal
- Collection communications should not reveal more detail about a patient’s care than necessary
- Patient statements should be addressed and delivered carefully to avoid disclosure to the wrong person

Patients also have rights, including access to their billing records and the ability to request restrictions on disclosures to their health plan when they pay in full out of pocket for a service.

## The Security Rule

The Security Rule requires administrative, physical, and technical safeguards for electronic PHI. For billing operations, key safeguards include:

- **Risk analysis:** A documented, regularly updated assessment of risks to electronic PHI. This is a foundational requirement and a frequent finding in enforcement actions when it is missing.
- **Access controls:** Unique user IDs, role-based permissions, and prompt removal of access when staff leave
- **Authentication:** Strong passwords and multi-factor authentication for systems containing PHI
- **Encryption:** For laptops, mobile devices, and data in transit
- **Audit logs:** Tracking who accessed what information
- **Workforce training** on security policies and recognizing phishing
- **Contingency planning:** Backups and the ability to restore systems after an outage or ransomware attack

## Breach notification

A **breach** is generally an impermissible use or disclosure of unsecured PHI that compromises its privacy or security. When a breach occurs:

- **Affected individuals** must be notified without unreasonable delay and no later than **60 days** after discovery
- **HHS** must be notified — within 60 days for breaches affecting 500 or more people, or within 60 days after the end of the calendar year for smaller breaches
- **Media notice** is required for breaches affecting more than 500 residents of a state or jurisdiction
- **Business associates** must notify the covered entity of breaches they discover

Many states have additional breach notification laws.

## Common risks in billing operations

- Phishing emails leading to compromised accounts
- Lost or stolen unencrypted laptops
- Sending statements or records to the wrong address or fax number
- Former employees retaining system access
- Sharing passwords between staff
- Using personal email or messaging apps to discuss patient accounts

## A practical compliance checklist

1. Complete and document a security risk analysis, and update it regularly
2. Maintain written privacy and security policies
3. Sign BAAs with every vendor that handles PHI
4. Train all staff at hire and at least annually
5. Use multi-factor authentication and encryption
6. Review user access regularly and remove it promptly when staff leave
7. Have an incident response and breach notification plan
8. Keep documentation for at least six years, as required by HIPAA
