Every practice eventually asks whether it should keep billing in-house or hand it to a specialist company. The decision is often made on instinct — "outsourcing is expensive" or "our biller is overwhelmed" — rather than on numbers.

This guide breaks down the real costs of both models, walks through a worked example, and explains the factors that matter beyond price. You can also try our [medical billing cost calculator](/tools/medical-billing-cost-calculator) with your own figures.

## The true cost of in-house billing

The biggest mistake practices make is comparing an outsourcing fee to a biller’s salary alone. A full picture includes:

### 1. Salaries

Experienced medical billers and coders in the United States commonly earn somewhere in the range of **$45,000 to $65,000 per year**, with certified coders and billing managers earning more. Rates vary significantly by region and experience.

### 2. Benefits and payroll taxes

Health insurance, retirement contributions, paid time off, and payroll taxes typically add **20% to 30%** on top of salary.

### 3. Software and clearinghouse fees

Practice management software, clearinghouse transaction fees, eligibility checking tools, coding references, and patient statement services can add **several hundred to a few thousand dollars per month**, depending on the size of the practice.

### 4. Training and certification

Coding rules, payer policies, and regulations change every year. Continuing education, certification fees, and conference attendance are ongoing costs.

### 5. Turnover and coverage gaps

When a biller leaves or takes extended leave, claims stop moving. Recruiting, onboarding, and the cash-flow dip while a new hire gets up to speed can easily cost more than a month of collections.

### 6. Management time

Someone — often the practice manager or a physician — must supervise billing, review reports, and handle escalations.

### 7. The cost of underperformance

This is the hidden cost. If a small team cannot keep up, denials go unworked, A/R ages past the point of collection, and underpayments are never noticed. A few percentage points of lost collections can outweigh every other cost on this list.

## How outsourced billing is priced

Most medical billing companies charge a **percentage of collections**, commonly in the range of **4% to 10%** depending on specialty, claim volume, and the scope of services. Others charge per claim or a flat monthly fee.

When comparing quotes, confirm exactly what is included:

- Charge entry and coding (or only claim submission?)
- Eligibility verification and prior authorization
- Denial management and appeals
- A/R follow-up on older claims
- Patient statements and patient call support
- Credentialing and reporting

A lower percentage that excludes denial management may cost more overall than a higher percentage that includes it.

## A worked example

Consider a practice that collects **$1,000,000 per year**.

**In-house model (illustrative):**

- Two billing staff at $52,000 each: $104,000
- Benefits and taxes at 25%: $26,000
- Software, clearinghouse, and statements: $18,000
- Training and certification: $3,000
- **Total: about $151,000 per year — roughly 15% of collections**

**Outsourced model (illustrative):**

- 6% of $1,000,000 in collections: **$60,000 per year**
- Some practices keep a part-time staff member for front-desk tasks, adding cost back in

In this example, outsourcing costs less in direct terms. But the result depends heavily on your numbers. A very large practice with an efficient, experienced in-house team may achieve a lower cost per dollar collected. A small practice with one biller may find outsourcing both cheaper and more resilient.

## Beyond cost: what really matters

### Collection performance

The most important number is not the fee — it is your **net collection rate** and **days in A/R**. A partner that improves collections by even 3–5% can pay for itself. See our guide to [revenue cycle KPIs](/resources/revenue-cycle-kpis).

### Visibility and control

Some practices worry about losing control when they outsource. Insist on regular, transparent reporting, access to your own practice management system, and clear service levels for claim submission and denial follow-up.

### Scalability

Adding a provider or location in-house means hiring. An outsourced partner can typically scale with your volume.

### Expertise

Specialty billing — such as surgery, behavioral health, or wound care — requires specific knowledge that a small in-house team may not have.

### Compliance and security

Any billing partner handling patient data must sign a business associate agreement and follow HIPAA safeguards. Ask how they control access, train staff, and protect data.

## A hybrid option

Many practices choose a middle path: keeping front-desk tasks such as scheduling and copay collection in-house while outsourcing claims, denials, and A/R follow-up. Others outsource only specific projects, such as cleaning up old A/R or credentialing new providers.

## How to decide

1. Calculate your fully loaded in-house cost as a percentage of collections.
2. Review your current KPIs: denial rate, days in A/R, and net collection rate.
3. Request detailed quotes that list included services.
4. Ask for references from practices in your specialty.
5. Compare the projected collections — not just the fee.
