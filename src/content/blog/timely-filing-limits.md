A **timely filing limit** is the deadline by which a claim must be submitted to a payer after the date of service. If a claim arrives after the deadline, the payer will deny it — usually with claim adjustment reason code **CO-29** (*the time limit for filing has expired*) — and, because it is a contractual adjustment, the patient generally cannot be billed.

Timely filing denials are entirely preventable, yet they remain a steady source of lost revenue in many practices.

## Typical timely filing limits

Limits vary widely, and they are set by law, regulation, and individual payer contracts.

- **Medicare (traditional):** Claims must generally be filed within **12 months (one calendar year)** of the date of service. There are limited exceptions, such as retroactive Medicare entitlement or administrative error.
- **Medicaid:** Deadlines are set by each state and its managed care plans and can range from about **90 days to one year**.
- **Medicare Advantage and commercial plans:** Commonly between **90 and 180 days** from the date of service, though some contracts allow longer. Your provider contract is the authoritative source.
- **Secondary claims:** Many payers set a separate deadline measured from the date the primary payer processed the claim.
- **Corrected claims and appeals:** These usually have their own deadlines, which may be shorter than the original filing limit.

Because limits differ so much, every practice should maintain a **payer reference sheet** listing the filing limit, corrected claim limit, and appeal deadline for each major payer.

## How claims miss the deadline

Most timely filing denials do not happen because a claim was forgotten entirely. They happen because:

1. **Claims were rejected and never resubmitted.** A clearinghouse or payer rejection means the claim never entered the payer’s system. If no one works the rejection report, the deadline passes quietly.
2. **Charges were entered late.** Unsigned notes, missing charge tickets, and backlogs in coding delay submission.
3. **Wrong payer.** A claim sent to an old or incorrect payer still runs out the clock with the correct one.
4. **Secondary claims were not filed** after the primary payer paid.
5. **Credentialing delays.** Practices may hold claims while waiting for a provider to be enrolled, and some never get billed in time.
6. **Corrected claims were sent too late** after a denial.

## How to protect every claim

### Submit fast

Set a target for **charge lag** — the time between the date of service and claim submission — of just a few days. Monitor it by provider and location.

### Work rejections daily

Review clearinghouse and payer rejection reports every business day. A rejected claim must be corrected and resubmitted; until it is, the payer has no record of it.

### Track claims with no response

Run a report of claims with no payer response after 30 days, and check their status. "No response" often means the payer never received the claim.

### Keep proof of timely filing

If you need to dispute a timely filing denial, you will need evidence. Acceptable proof typically includes clearinghouse acceptance reports or payer acknowledgment reports showing the claim was received before the deadline. Screenshots of your own system alone are usually not enough.

### Plan around credentialing

Know whether each payer allows retroactive billing for newly credentialed providers and how far back. Submit claims as soon as enrollment is effective. Our [guide to Medicare credentialing in Florida](/resources/medicare-credentialing-florida) explains effective dates in more detail.

### Monitor aging A/R

Claims aged over 60 or 90 days with no payment deserve immediate attention, both because they are close to deadlines and because they become harder to collect. See our article on [A/R follow-up](/resources/ar-follow-up-best-practices).

## Appealing a timely filing denial

A timely filing denial can sometimes be overturned when:

- You can prove the claim was received by the payer within the limit
- The payer’s own error caused the delay
- The patient provided incorrect insurance information and you can document your efforts
- Retroactive eligibility or coordination of benefits issues delayed the claim

Submit the appeal with clear documentation and a short explanation. If the denial was correct, write off the claim and identify the process failure so it does not happen again.

## The bottom line

Timely filing is a process problem, not a payer problem. Practices that submit quickly, work rejections daily, and track unacknowledged claims rarely lose revenue to filing deadlines.
